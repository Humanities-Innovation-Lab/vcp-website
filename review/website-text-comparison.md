## Intro (homepage)

### Original

### Humanities Data Inquiry
Humanities Data Inquiry (HDI) studies how researchers in the “traditional” Humanities produce, use, and share the materials that information professionals call data—and why the digital tools, policies, and infrastructure built to manage research data have been so slowly adopted across the Humanities.
Most efforts to close this gap ask how Humanities researchers should change to fit contemporary Research Data Management (RDM) and Research Data Infrastructure (RDI). HDI turns the question around. We begin from the premise that the Humanities already have long-standing, robust—if often intuitive and unspoken—traditions for collecting, organizing, preserving, and circulating research materials. Our goal is to understand those traditions on their own terms, and to use that understanding to make data management and infrastructure work better for the Humanities, rather than the other way around.
Hosted by the University of Lethbridge Humanities Innovation Lab, HDI combines historical, ethnographic, and comparative research to build a clearer, evidence-based picture of Humanities data practice—past and present.
Learn more

### Current

### Humanities Data Inquiry
Humanities Data Inquiry (HDI) is a multi-phase interdisciplinary research program examining the digital transformation of humanities research. Established in 2021 and continuing through 2029, the project investigates how humanities researchers and information professionals — including archivists, librarians, curators, technologists, and cultural heritage professionals — design, build, govern, and sustain research and cultural heritage infrastructure in an era of rapidly expanding digital and computational research.
Drawing together ethnographic, historical, comparative, and infrastructure-focused approaches, HDI examines the changing relationships among research practice, data, preservation, governance, ethics, and long-term stewardship within the humanities and cultural heritage ecosystem.

## Research Program

### Original

### Research Program
Across much of the Natural, Social, and Health Sciences, improved research data management and infrastructure have reshaped day-to-day practice. In the Humanities, that transformation has largely been confined to Digital Humanities (DH) and to Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums (GLAM). For the broader Humanities community, open-data policies and infrastructure are often seen as irrelevant, or even as a threat.
This is usually explained as a problem within the Humanities—a lack of training, awareness, or willingness to modernize. HDI proposes a different reading. We treat this “resistance to data” not as stubbornness or neglect but as an implicit methodological argument: a reflection of how Humanities researchers actually understand the role and nature of their research materials.
To test that idea, HDI asks questions of continuity rather than change—how researchers have traditionally understood research materials such as primary and secondary sources, artefacts, facsimiles, models, transcriptions, and compendia; what techniques and infrastructures they have developed to manage and share them; and how today’s networked infrastructure can accommodate these well-developed traditions.
HDI uses an interdisciplinary, mixed-methods design with an ethnographic framing, combining semi-structured interviews and observation with historical and comparative case studies. The research is grounded in fieldwork with active scholars and with the information professionals—archivists, librarians, curators, data curators, software engineers, and others—who build and sustain digital research collections.
This work contributes to scholarly dialogue on
- The nature of “data” in the Humanities (and where the term fits awkwardly)
- Humanities research practices and data cultures
- Research Data Management and Research Data Infrastructure policy
- The history of Humanities research and of scholarly editing
- Digital collections, GLAM digitization, and collaborative scholarship
- How research infrastructure can better serve Humanities work

### Current

### Research Program
HDI uses a multi-method research approach combining ethnographic interviews and participant observation, comparative case analysis, systematic mapping reviews of the literature, historical research, and collaborative engagement with researchers and information professionals.
The project examines how humanities scholars work across diverse disciplinary, institutional, and cultural heritage contexts, and how their practices shape — and are shaped by — the design, governance, and long-term stewardship of research and cultural heritage infrastructure. Rather than asking how researchers should adapt to existing infrastructure, HDI turns the question around, beginning from the premise that the humanities already have robust, if often intuitive, traditions for producing, preserving, and circulating the materials information professionals call “data” — and asking how infrastructure can be built to support them.
The research unfolds across three connected strands: a historical strand tracing how editions, transcriptions, facsimiles, and other representations of cultural objects have long been collected and shared — an early, intuitive form of data infrastructure; an ethnographic strand documenting how scholars across fields like literary studies, art history, archaeology, classics, and history work with their materials; and a comparative strand examining how digital collections are designed through collaboration between researchers and infrastructure professionals.
This work contributes to scholarly dialogue on
- Humanities research practices and data cultures
- Digital cultural heritage infrastructure
- Governance, stewardship, and sustainability of digital collections
- Research data management and policy
- Cultural data ethics and community authority
- The broader implications of the digital transformation of the human cultural record

## Program Areas

### Original

### Research Streams
The current SSHRC Insight phase of HDI (2025–2029) runs three connected lines of inquiry. Together they build a picture of Humanities data practice that is historical as well as contemporary, descriptive as well as comparative.
### Historical & Theoretical
### Representational data and the long history of Humanities “data”
This stream traces how Humanities researchers have historically collected, managed, and disseminated what RDM/RDI specialists would call data—beginning with “representational data”: the transcriptions, facsimiles, editions, models, recordings, and photographs that stand in for cultural objects and activities.
Viewed this way, a critical edition is itself a form of data infrastructure—it gathers representations of cultural objects, attaches detailed metadata, and presents them for further analysis. The stream asks how those long-standing practices compare to the assumptions built into today’s data infrastructure.
### Ethnographic
### How Humanists actually work
Through an intensive interview program with active scholars (target n≈150) across fields such as literary and cultural studies, art history, archaeology, classics, and history, this stream documents how researchers form their questions, work with sources and digital tools, and think about research materials as research materials.
Sampling deliberately balances scholars who work with non-digital materials and those who work digitally, in order to capture both the variety and the common ground across the Humanities.
### Comparative
### Designing digital collections
This stream extends HDI’s comparative case analysis to the design of digital thematic research collections—sites of intensive collaboration between researchers and infrastructure professionals.
Using a “user-ecology” perspective, it examines how collections are designed to serve many kinds of users, and how that collaboration shapes new modes of computational research and digital publishing in the Humanities.

### Current

### Program Areas
HDI is structured across three overlapping areas of inquiry spanning humanities research practices, infrastructure development, and governance.
### 2021–2024
### Foundations and FAIR Humanities Data
The initial SSHRC Partnership Development Grant focused on the problems and opportunities associated with Open and FAIR humanities data practices, including semantic publication workflows, repository infrastructure, and sustainable dissemination models for humanities and cultural heritage research.
This work examined how humanities “small data” could participate in emerging digital and computational ecosystems without sacrificing interpretive depth, disciplinary specificity, or long-standing scholarly practices.
### 2025–2029
### Humanities Research Practices and Data Ecologies
This area of the project expanded into ethnographic, comparative, and historical study of humanities research practices, data use, and infrastructure design.
Research in this area examines how humanities researchers, information professionals, and cultural institutions conceptualize, manage, and sustain research materials, digital collections, and scholarly infrastructure across diverse disciplinary and organizational settings.
### 2025–2029
### Digital Research and Heritage Infrastructure, Governance, Stewardship, and Policy
This area of the project examines the governance, ethics, sustainability, and long-term stewardship of digital research and cultural heritage infrastructure.
Research focuses on institutional collaboration, infrastructure design, community authority, cultural data ethics, and policy development related to digital heritage and research infrastructure.

### Outputs & Collaborations
HDI is led from the Humanities Innovation Lab at the University of Lethbridge and carried out through collaboration with Humanities scholars and with the information professionals and cultural institutions who build research infrastructure. Fieldwork and case studies draw on universities and GLAM organizations (Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums).
Project outputs include
- Research articles and comparative case studies
- Historical studies of Humanities data practice and scholarly editing
- Ethnographic accounts of contemporary Humanities research practice
- Conference presentations and disciplinary dissemination

- Recommendations to help RDM/RDI better serve Humanities researchers

HDI is conducted in partnership with the Humanities Innovation Lab and the Lethbridge Journal Incubator at the University of Lethbridge, and through collaborations with researchers, cultural institutions, infrastructure organizations, and community partners internationally. - Open-access and community-oriented digital scholarship initiatives
The work is meant to serve humanities researchers, the information professionals who design research infrastructure, and the institutions, students, and publics who rely on humanities scholarship.

## About the Project

### Original

### About the Project
The challenge. Across the research enterprise, better tools for managing and sharing data have changed how scholarship gets done—but largely not in the Humanities outside of Digital Humanities (DH) and GLAM (Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums). Why the Humanities have been slow to adopt Research Data Management (RDM) and Research Data Infrastructure (RDI) is a live question in research policy, information science, and Digital Humanities, and one of little apparent interest to most Humanities researchers themselves.
Our approach. Rather than treating this as a deficit to be corrected, Resistance to Data investigates the data practices of “traditional” Humanities researchers on their own terms—through historical, ethnographic, and comparative study—and uses what it finds to identify where data policy and infrastructure could better serve them. In day-to-day work the project often uses the phrase “research materials” to bridge the gap between how Humanities researchers and information professionals describe the same things.
A note on “traditional.” We use “traditional Humanities” as a working term, not a synonym for “non-digital.” Defining it more precisely—and understanding the wide variety of data practices it contains—is itself one of the project’s research questions.
